A Fast Layer Under the Game Master: Small Models That Keep a Galaxy Alive

How small, cheap models could run the everyday life of the galaxy (captains, docks, stations, rumors, pirates, traders and factions) inside rules the AI game master sets, what other games learned doing this, and which OpenRouter models, prices and structured outputs fit. The game is a native desktop app that always assumes internet, and this layer runs only on OpenRouter, with no local model.

Two layers, two speeds

The game master writes the rules of life; the fast layer lives it

The slow, strong AI game master works through the live scripting engine on arcs, new systems, eras and Ancients canon. Under it, fast and cheap models make the many small decisions that make the galaxy feel inhabited: what a captain says at the dock, how a station reacts to a shortage, which rumor travels, whether a pirate attacks, how a faction nudges a tariff. Each fast decision is made inside a brief and a budget the game master set, chosen from allowed actions, checked, applied by ordinary game code, and written back to world history so the game master can see what the galaxy did.

  1. AI game master (slow, strong) Actor

    Arcs, systems, eras, canon. Writes NPC briefs, allowed actions, price bands and budgets through the scripting engine.

  2. ↓ briefs and rules
  3. World state and history log

    Base engine holds state, invariants and validators.

  4. ↓
  5. Something happens

    You dock, a ware runs short, a convoy passes, a daily tick.

  6. ↓ grounding packet
  7. Fast layer decides Decision

    Utility AI pre-scores options; a small OpenRouter model or a decision model such as TypeSafe Jev picks one or writes a short line.

  8. ↓ typed choice
  9. Validator

    Ids exist, rules allow it, inside the band and the budget.

    ↺ back to Fast layer decides: fail: retry once, or skip that moment

  10. ↓ pass
  11. Applied and logged Outcome

    Prices, routes, offers and lines change; the outcome is written to history.

    ↺ back to AI game master (slow, strong): digests of what changed

Recommended starting setup

  1. 1
    Pre-filter: utility AI

    Rule-based scoring (Infinite Axis style) narrows each decision to the top few allowed options before any model sees it, and handles background ships nobody is watching.

  2. 2
    Typed choices: TypeSafe Jev on OpenRouter

    Pirate, trader and station choices, yes/no and scores go to Jev through the Decisions API: $0.042 per million input tokens, output free, no text to check.

  3. 3
    Short text: a cheap OpenRouter model with structured output

    Dock lines, notice boards and rumors on openai/gpt-oss-20b ($0.018 input, $0.09 output per million tokens) with a JSON schema. Step up to mistral-small-3.2-24b or claude-haiku-5.5 where the writing needs to be better.

  4. 4
    Constrained choice, not free text

    The model returns an action id from the allowed list plus a few typed fields; only dialogue lines are free text, and even those are length-capped and checked.

  5. 5
    Native desktop app, always online

    The game always assumes internet and the fast layer uses only OpenRouter: no local model, no bundled runtime, no share of the player's GPU. A failed or slow call is retried once or that moment is skipped.

This is a starting point to test, not a measured result for this game: measure call latency and cost per play session with the real game before choosing models.

What 'jev' is

Real: TypeSafe Jev, a decision model on OpenRouter, not a chat model

Jev is a structured decision model from TypeSafe, listed on OpenRouter. It does not write text. You give it options and it returns probabilities: which option to pick, how likely yes is, or where something sits on a scale. That fits pirate, trader and station choices well. It runs only on OpenRouter, which is where this layer lives anyway.

Model ids
typesafe/jev-1.14 (released 14 October 2026), ~typesafe/jev-latest, and typesafe/jev-1.13 still available. A related typesafe/jev-router uses Jev to pick a model per request.
Price and limits
$0.042 per million input tokens, output free, 262,144-token context. The model page lists latency of 0.11 seconds.
Decision types
Choice (one option, with probabilities and confidence), yes/no probability, Score (position on an ordered scale), and Box (an image region, Jev 1.14 only). No text and no explanations.
How it is called
Not through chat completions. OpenRouter's Decisions API (POST /api/alpha/decisions) or the System One API (POST /api/v1/systemone). Requests over about 32,000 bytes or with images use a slower, less accurate long-context mode, so keep the grounding packet small.
The wider category
OpenRouter lists 22 decision models from 13 makers as of 16 October 2026, among them OpenAI, Microsoft, Perplexity, Cloudflare, Inception, Liquid and Upstage models.
Sources
OpenRouter Jev guide · Jev 1.14 model page · TypeSafe models docs

What earlier projects learned

Each entry describes a kind of project, what worked, what went wrong and what it means for this game. Entries are described by pattern rather than by name. Review shares are English-language user reviews from store pages.

LLM characters in games · 13

A voice-chat AI teammate in an online shooter

What it is
An AI teammate you talk to by voice, built with NVIDIA ACE. The beta ran 17 June to 1 July 2026 on a 2B on-device small language model plus speech recognition and speech synthesis.
What worked
A two-speed design: the language model makes high-level choices and a faster control layer carries them out in the match. The publisher reports data from about 39,000 sessions, with positive answers ahead of negative by 25.1 points on whether players would recommend the teammate.
What went wrong
Hardware gate: the minimum is an RTX 2080 Ti or 3060 with 8 GB of VRAM, so the feature excludes part of the audience, and the model shares the GPU with the game.
For this game
The closest published match to this page's idea: a small model steers, ordinary game code acts. Keep the model off the frame-critical path.
Sources
arXiv 2609.29837

On-device NPC thoughts in a life sim

What it is
A life sim (built with NVIDIA ACE) where NPC thoughts and topic choices come from a 0.5B Mistral-NeMo-Minitron model running on the player's RTX GPU, applied only to characters in visible range.
What worked
Fully on-device and cheap: about 1 GB of VRAM. Scoping the model to nearby characters is a sensible budget rule. The game overall sits at Mostly Positive, about 79% of user reviews, in Early Access since March 2025.
What went wrong
Players report that the AI mostly shows up as thought bubbles and changes little of what characters actually do, plus bugs and frame-rate dips in crowds. RTX-only.
For this game
If model output does not change prices, routes, offers or relationships, players stop noticing it. Visible consequences matter more than clever text.

A voice-driven detective tech demo (Inworld with NVIDIA ACE)

What it is
A GDC 2024 tech demo: you play a detective questioning hotel NPCs by voice, with speech recognition and facial animation from NVIDIA ACE.
What worked
Showed that a full speech-in, speech-out character loop can run in a playable scene.
What went wrong
It stayed a demo. Inworld has since shifted toward general AI infrastructure and a code-first runtime rather than a shipped game.
For this game
Conversation is the easy part to demo. The design work is making talk change world state in ways the rules allow.
Sources

A voice-chat NPC mod for open-world RPGs

What it is
A mod that lets you talk to any NPC by voice, with cloud or local LLMs.
What worked
Supports local models fully offline and documents a target of under 0.5 seconds to first response for real-time feel.
What went wrong
Real-world local latency is often worse: one user reported 1 to 3 seconds with a local 13B model on an RTX 3090. Value is mostly dialogue rather than world change.
For this game
Latency budgets for live talk are tight. Decisions that are not conversation can run ahead of time where seconds do not matter.

Generative Agents (Smallville)

What it is

Believable emergent social behavior, such as agents spreading word of a party and showing up.

Failure modes and how to avoid them

Failure Seen in Design answer
Cost Generative Agents Small, cheap OpenRouter models; call a model only on events, not every tick; per-region budgets.
Latency Voice-chat NPC mods, a hardware AI companion Decide ahead of time where possible; nothing waits on the model; a late call skips that moment.
Made-up facts Free-form text adventure drift Grounding packet of facts by id; output may only reference those ids.
Sameness Uniform utility weights Per-NPC temperament from the game master; memory of the player; vary the facts, not just the words.
Players ignore it Life-sim thought bubbles Every decision changes something you can see: a price, a route, an offer, a relationship.
A weaker second path A companion mod's offline mode One path only: OpenRouter, always online; no separate local mode to maintain.
GPU contention Life-sim frame dips, an 8 GB minimum for an AI teammate No model on the player's GPU: the fast layer runs on OpenRouter, with calls batched at quiet moments such as docking.

Architecture patterns

The recurring lesson across the projects above: a model is safest and most useful when it chooses among moves the game already understands, sees only the facts that matter now, and leaves a record the world can remember.

Choose from allowed actions

  1. Event

    Shortage of water at Hollin Dock.

  2. ↓
  3. Rules list allowed actions

    raise_buy_price, post_request, ration, ask_neighbor. Each with preconditions and limits from the brief.

  4. ↓
  5. Utility AI scores them

    Keeps the top three or four for the model.

    → Apply through game code: background ships, or when the region's model budget is used up

  6. ↓ top options plus personality
  7. OpenRouter model picks Decision

    A small model with a JSON schema, or Jev returning probabilities: an action id from the list plus typed fields.

  8. ↓ valid choice
  9. Apply through game code Outcome
Method What it guarantees Where
JSON schema Output parses and matches the schema, including enums OpenRouter structured outputs (model-level flag; can vary by provider)
Tool calling The model names one tool and its arguments Most OpenRouter models
Decision model A probability per option; no text at all TypeSafe Jev and other OpenRouter decision models
Free generation Nothing; use only for short lines, then check them Any OpenRouter text model

Grounding and write-back

  1. Gather the grounding packet

    The NPC's brief, the current state with ids, and the few history entries that involve this NPC, this station or the player. Retrieved by id and tags, like trigger-word story cards in generated text adventures.

  2. ↓
  3. Decide or speak

    The model may reference only ids in the packet.

  4. ↓
  5. Write an event record

    Who, what, where, which ids, a reason tag, and the brief version that allowed it.

  6. ↓
  7. World history log

    Feeds future packets, rumors and the 'while you were away' digest.

  8. ↓ compressed summaries
  9. Game master reads digests Actor

    Spots trends, adjusts briefs and bands, starts arcs from what the galaxy actually did.

    ↺ back to Gather the grounding packet: updated briefs

The game master's brief for each NPC

Field What the game master sets Example
Goals What this NPC wants this era Pay off the freighter; get revenge on the Varn clan
Temperament A few numeric traits Greed 0.7, caution 0.3, warmth 0.5
Allowed actions The moves this NPC may make Trade, tip, refuse, hail, hire escort
Limits Bands and budgets Prices within 15% of base; at most one hostile act per week
Knowledge Which facts and rumors they can know Local market, two rumors, your last three visits
Voice Speech style and length cap Dry, short sentences, at most 25 words
Off limits Topics only the game master may touch Ancients canon, new factions, new named characters

Validation

  1. 1
    Shape

    Parses, matches the schema, action id is in the allowed list.

  2. 2
    References

    Every id exists and was in the grounding packet; no new named people, places or ships.

  3. 3
    Rules

    Preconditions hold in the current state, which may have changed since the request.

  4. 4
    Limits

    Inside the brief's bands and the region's budget.

  5. 5
    Text

    Length cap, English only, no canon terms the brief forbids.

  6. 6
    On failure

    Drop the result, retry once at most or skip that moment, and log the failure for tuning. Never retry in a loop.

When a call fails

The game always assumes internet. If an OpenRouter call fails or comes back late, retry once or skip that moment; nothing in the world waits on it. This is an engineering detail, not a separate game mode.

Budgets, caching and batching

  • Event-driven, not per tick Budget

    Call a model when something happens to someone who matters, not on a clock for every ship. Background ships use utility AI only.

  • Spend near the player Budget

    Like the life sim's visible-range rule: model decisions for the station you are docked at and ships you can see; rules elsewhere.

  • Batch at quiet moments Batching

    Docking and jumps are natural pauses: prepare the next station's chatter and reactions in one batch.

  • Cache by situation Caching

    Same NPC, same facts, same choice set gives the same answer; reuse it. Keep the brief as a stable prompt prefix so providers that support prompt caching can reuse it.

  • Rate limits per region Rate limit

    A cap on model calls per region per in-game day, set by the game master. When used up, minor moments in that region take the top-scored option or stay quiet.

  • Pre-generated lines Reuse

    Keep generated lines that passed validation as a growing pool keyed by facts and tone, so later moments can reuse them.

What 1,000 decisions cost (arithmetic on listed prices)

Latency classes (estimate)
OpenRouter's endpoint latency fields were empty when checked, so only Jev's listed 0.11 s is a published figure. Expect small cloud models to answer in a fraction of a second to a few seconds including the network, varying by provider and load.
Why OpenRouter only
One API for small LLMs and decision models, models swapped by id, and no runtime or GPU cost on the player's PC. The game always assumes internet; a failed or slow call is retried once or that moment is skipped.

Why not a local model

Decided: OpenRouter only, no local model

The game is a native desktop app that always assumes internet, so there is no reason to carry a model on the player's PC. A local model would mean bundling a runtime and model files, sharing the GPU with the game, and gating the feature on hardware, which is much harder than calling OpenRouter.

Context from games that did run models on the player's hardware (see the entries above): the shooter's AI teammate needs at least an 8 GB card and shares the GPU with the game, the life sim's 0.5B model takes about 1 GB of graphics memory, and users of a hardware AI companion and a voice-chat NPC mod saw multi-second delays even on strong GPUs.

Menu: what the fast layer could do

Each idea is calm (no timers), reacts to your history, never scales to your level, and needs no approval inbox. The tag says what kind of output it needs.

Docks and captains · 4

  • Dock chatter that knows you Short text

    A captain's line on docking draws on facts from your history at this station: the ore you sold last time, the convoy you helped, the debt you still owe. Facts come from the world log by id, so the line can only mention what happened. Authored lines keyed on the same facts stay in the mix.

  • Haggling stance Choice

    When you trade with a named captain, the model picks one stance from an allowed set (generous, firm, wary, desperate) based on their personality and their record with you. Prices stay inside the band the game master set; the stance only picks the point in the band and the tone.

  • Tip or silence Yes / no

    A captain decides whether to share a tip about a rich field or a quiet route, based on how you treated them before. Remembered goodwill becomes useful information, not a reward screen.

  • Hails in passing Choice

    Passing ships choose to greet, ignore, warn or ask for help, from an allowed list and your reputation with their faction. Calm by default: no forced stops, no timers.

Stations and shortages · 3

  • Shortage reactions Choice

    When stock of a ware falls below a threshold, the station picks among allowed responses: raise its buy price within the band, post a supply request on the notice board, ration sales, or ask a neighboring station. Different stations react differently because their briefs differ.

  • Station mood Score

    A station's mood on an ordered scale, from thriving to strained, scored from its recent history. Mood feeds prices, chatter and which requests appear.

  • Notice board in the station's voice Short text

    Requests and announcements written from the real state of the station: what it lacks, who it trusts, what happened last week. Text only; the request itself is created by rules.

Rumors · 3

  • Rumors from real events Short text

    Events in the world log become rumors told at bars and over comms. The game master sets how much each rumor may distort and which ones may be false; the fast model only phrases and spreads them.

  • Who tells you what Choice

    An NPC picks which rumor to share from the ones they know, weighted by relationship and personality. A friend shares what helps you; a rival shares what helps them.

  • Rumor decay Score

    How stale a rumor has become as it travels, scored per hop. Old rumors get vaguer; the facts behind them do not change.

Pirates and traders · 3

  • Trader route choice Choice

    Trader captains pick among known profitable routes using their own risk tolerance and what they have heard. The result is an economy with personalities, not a uniform optimizer.

  • Pirate decisions Choice

    A pirate picks to shadow, demand toll, attack or leave, within an aggression budget the game master sets per region. Never scaled to the player's level or wealth; tied to history, such as a pirate you spared before.

  • Escorts and convoys Choice

    Traders decide whether to hire an escort or group up after news of piracy, which changes where the safe lanes are.

Faction micro-moves · 2

  • Daily small moves Choice

    Patrol placement, a tariff nudge within band, a minor gesture to a neighbor. The game master sets each faction's strategic stance and limits; the fast layer fills in the daily texture.

  • Reaction to your reputation Score

    How warm a faction office is with you, scored from your record: discounts, docking priority, or a cold shoulder.

Mining and the log · 2

  • Prospectors and depletion Choice

    NPC miners notice depleted fields and move on, claim new ones, or compete with you, leaving visible traces in the world.

  • While you were away Short text

    A short ship-log digest of what changed since your last visit to a region, written only from logged events. It explains the evolving world without an inbox or approvals.

What stays with the game master

Fast layer

  • Individual captain, pirate and trader choices
  • Station reactions within price bands
  • Dock chatter, hails and notice-board wording

Risks and open questions

  • Call latency Measure

    Open: how long do OpenRouter calls take from a player's PC with the chosen providers? Needs measuring with the real game; batching at docking keeps waits out of play.

  • Sameness creeping back Risk

    Small models fall into stock phrasing. Mitigation: vary facts and temperament, cap reuse of a line, and keep authored lines in the mix.

  • Drift from canon Risk

    A fast model could hint at Ancients lore. The brief forbids it and the validator checks for canon terms.

  • Players not noticing Risk

    If decisions do not change visible things, the work is wasted, as the life-sim thought bubbles show. Every menu item should move a price, route, offer or relationship.

  • Determinism and saves Question

    Open: should a reload give the same decisions? Caching by situation and logging choices makes replays consistent.

  • Models and prices change Question

    OpenRouter's list moves. Keep models chosen by id in config and the typed interface stable, so one model can replace another.

  • Cost and privacy Question

    Who pays for OpenRouter calls, and what world data leaves the PC? A hard monthly cap and a clear setting are needed.

  • Jev's future Risk

    Decision models on OpenRouter are new; the Decisions API path is marked alpha. Keep the same typed interface so another OpenRouter decision model, or a small model with a JSON schema, can replace it.
