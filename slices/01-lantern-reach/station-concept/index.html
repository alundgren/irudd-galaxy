Station concept: a station that can grow

Three ways to build a station out of parts, so Lantern Reach can start with a market and a hullwright and later hold ten or more rooms without feeling cramped. The game master adds, alters and removes those parts as data. Direction agreed in review: proposal A, "The station up close", with proposal C's news cards folded into its station key. Build its hullwright view first: that decision is already made, and the hullwright is the weakest screen today. The last section, Dreaming ahead, looks past the slice at how that station could become a living place, with semi-3D art, animation and a theme for each system.

What this page is. A design and art-direction exploration. Nothing here changes the Rust slice. Every mock is drawn in the page as SVG in the warm sketched style of the art bible (warm ink, hatching, lamp glow, faction paint). Numbers in the mocks are illustrative and get tuned in play.

Same in all three

The problem from playtests: the dock is one cream panel, and everything a station offers is a row in it. The hullwright is a flat text line. A station with ten things to do would be a scroll of rows. All three proposals start from the same idea and differ in how the player sees and moves between the parts.

  • A station is a list of parts. Each part is a room of a kind the code already knows how to show: dockmaster, market, wharf (hullwright), notice wall, canteen, greenhouse, berths, and a general counter (a keeper, a list of offers, a few talk lines) that covers most new shops. The game master writes parts as data. Only a brand-new kind of room needs code.
  • Every part has a keeper. A person, so every change has someone to talk about it, which is the existing legibility rule (a place and a person).
  • Talk stays short. Dock talk is bubbles over whoever speaks, click to advance. The log is one small icon in the bottom-left corner. No proposal adds a text panel that grows with history.
  • Mouse first. Every room is reachable by clicking. W A S D always belongs to the ship and flies off.
  • Readable. Text is pure black on paper or pure white on sky, never grey. State colours (hover, drop target, over budget, gain, loss) come from a colourblind-safe set (orange, sky blue, vermilion, blue) and always pair with a shape, an arrow or a word.
  • Calm changes. Game master changes to parts follow the existing rules: applied while docked or paused, one big change per region, a rollback line, and nothing taken from the player.

The fit model (shared by all three)

The hullwright decisions from the issue, made concrete enough to draw. The three proposals frame this differently, but the rules are the same.

  • Sockets. The Wren, seen from above, has four: drill mount (nose), utility (behind the cockpit), hold (belly) and engine (tail). The utility socket carries a small control rack with room for every control module, plus one larger utility part.
  • Parts change the look. The lens sits on the nose, hold pods hang off the sides, swift fins sweep back from the tail. A glance at the ship in flight tells you its fit.
  • Two budgets. Power (lamp pips) caps what can run at once. Mass (weights) beyond what the hull carries freely costs top speed and turn rate. This makes fits a choice of style, such as a hauler (pods, steady engine, slow turns) or a prospector (lens or long-throw drill, seam lamp, swift fins), not one ladder.
  • Drag to fit. Drag a part onto a glowing socket. Sockets that can take it light up with a dashed sky-blue ring, and the stat preview shows the change before you let go. The hullwright then fits it with a short animation (about a second and a half, click to skip): the crane swings over, lowers the part, three bolt flashes, the hull dips and settles.
  • Nothing is lost. A part you take off goes into a locker that belongs to the hull, or to its lineage once hulls have upgrade trees. It does not belong to the station. Each ship's spares stay with that ship, so the locker doesn't turn into one heap across many ships, and you can open it at any wharf that works on that hull. (Today fittings are never removed. Swapping is needed once fits are tradeoffs, so the locker keeps the "nothing lost" promise.)
  • Controls are modules. Nav chip, rock cradle, docking clamp, order slate, loop log and the lane chart (autopilot) are small physical modules in the control rack. The basic controls (click to fly, click to work, map travel, queues and loops) use no budget and are never taken away: every hull has a rack for them, and they move across at prestige as they do today.
  • Bigger automation is per hull. Later automation, such as drones, needs a bay that only some hulls have, and it competes for power like any other part. Choosing a hull then also means choosing what kind of automation it carries.
  • Where parts come from. Three tabs at every wharf: Ready (pay credits), Make (materials plus a blueprint found in the world) and Friends (the hullwright's contacts at other stations send a part after you dock there; counted in dockings, never time).

A. The station up close

Docking zooms the camera into the station itself. Each part is a real module on the station, and you click the module to step into it.

1. Structure

  • The docked view is the station drawn large, from above, in the same style as flight: the pier, the berths, your ship moored. Each part is a module on it (the market booth, the wharf's cradle arm, the canteen dome, the notice mast) with a hanging sign.
  • Hover a module: it lifts a little, gets an orange outline, and its sign says who keeps it and one line of what it offers. Click: the camera glides to it (under half a second) and the room opens as a panel beside it. The station stays visible, and the panel never covers more than about 45% of the screen.
  • The station key is a row of icons along the bottom, one per part, for players who would rather not hunt. Right-click, Esc or the back button returns to the whole station.
  • The dockmaster's bubbles rise from the office; a keeper's bubbles rise from their module.

2. Growth

  • Parts bolt onto anchors on the station's asset-kit recipe (arms, rings, pier slots). Adding a part adds a module where people can see it, which is how Kettle Dock's greenhouse rings already grow.
  • Outpost (2 to 4 parts): the camera frames the whole station; one click reaches any part.
  • Hub (10 or more parts): parts group into quarters (a ring, an arm, a row of booths), each with one sign that lists its count. Click a quarter, the camera moves to it, click a part. Never more than two clicks, and the station key (grouped by quarter) is always one.
  • Size is held in bounds by the art bible's existing budget (at most 48 parts, a cap per kind), so a hub cannot sprawl past what the camera can frame.

3. Game master hooks

A part is a module plus a room. The game master writes it into a station-parts store, the validator checks it, and it appears at the next calm moment.

# One part of a station, written by core or by a game master script.
[[station.lantern_wharf.part]]
id      = "part.wharf.canteen"
room    = "canteen"            # a room kind the code knows
module  = "dome"               # asset-kit kind; the art bible's budget applies
anchor  = "pier.north.4"       # a free anchor on the station recipe
quarter = "pier"               # hubs only: which quarter sign it sits under
keeper  = "npc.bel_ashby"      # example keeper; must have a line citing the cause
state   = "building"           # open | closed | building
level   = 1                    # upgrades swap module parameters and widen offers
sign    = "Canteen · opens when Kettle ships rations"
cause   = { script = "gm.kettle_growth", facts = ["stage_built:kettle_dock:2"] }
rollback_line = "We've taken the canteen frame down to re-seal it."
  • A new shop opens: add a part with state = "building" (scaffold module, dashed sign), then "open".
  • The wharf closes for repairs: set state = "closed". The cradle gets a scaffold overlay and a sign ("Cradle down for re-tarring · Orrin fits at Kettle Dock"); the key icon shows a closed lamp; Orrin's line explains. Your locker stays yours.
  • A part is upgraded: raise level. A bigger cradle, more offers, a new line from the keeper.
  • Validator: the anchor exists and is free; the kind is within budget; there is a keeper with a line citing the cause; changes apply while docked or paused.

4. Art direction

5. Hullwright fit

  • The wharf is the cradle arm you can see on the station. Clicking it zooms the camera into the cradle, and your ship is lifted into it. There is no separate "shop screen": the drydock is a place on the station.
  • Drag from the tray onto a socket; the crane on the arm does the fitting animation where you can see it, and the ship is already wearing the part when you zoom back out to the whole station.
  • The tray has the three tabs (Ready, Make, Friends). A Friends part shows the person and station it comes from, and arrives after your next docking there.
  • Places differ through data: a wharf room lists the sockets it can work on and its own parts. A rough rigger at Cinder Hook might only do drill mounts, with heavier, cheaper parts and a smaller cradle module.

6. Risks, and what to test first

  • Click targets at hub size. Small modules on a big station get hard to hit. Quarters and the key are the answer, but they need testing.
  • Art and layout are coupled. A game master part needs a free anchor and a module that reads from above. Every room kind needs a module, which is more art than the other proposals.
  • Camera moves can feel slow on the tenth visit. Keep them short and skippable (a click during the glide lands at once).
  • Test first: the hullwright cradle view at the Wharf with the drag, the preview and the animation; then a mock 12-part hub against the Wharf, timing clicks to a named room and counting misclicks.

B. The quay

Docking opens a street of rooms. Each part is a shopfront on a boardwalk along the station's edge, and the room you are in fills the screen above it.

1. Structure

  • The docked view is a quay: shopfronts side by side along a boardwalk, seen from above, with your ship moored at the water's edge (the space side). The dockmaster's stand is always the first front, next to your ship.
  • Click a front and you step in: the room fills the upper two-thirds of the screen, at full width. The quay stays as a strip of rooftops along the bottom, so you can hop to the neighbour in one click.
  • Long quays scroll: drag the boardwalk, or click "more" at its end. The mouse wheel also pans it.
  • The keeper's portrait sits behind the counter, and their bubbles come from there.

2. Growth

  • A room takes one or two fronts of width. The quay is as long as its rooms.
  • Outpost: a short stub of pier with two or three fronts. No scrolling.
  • Hub: the quay is longer than the screen. It splits into named sections (Barge row, Lamp lane, Brass booths) shown as pills along the top of the strip; clicking a pill pans there. A very big hub can have two quays, an upper and a lower.
  • Empty lots ("To let") are part of the look: a station with space to grow shows it.

3. Game master hooks

# One room on a station's quay.
[[station.brassmouth_exchange.room]]
id       = "room.brassmouth.chandlery"
kind     = "counter"           # general shop: keeper, offers, talk lines
section  = "barge_row"
after    = "room.brassmouth.market"   # its place along the quay
frontage = 1                   # 1 or 2 widths
awning   = "faction"           # paint from the owner's swatch
keeper   = "npc.tamsin_oddway"
state    = "open"              # open | shuttered | lot
offers   = ["part.hold_pods", "ware.plate"]
cause    = { script = "gm.chandlery", facts = ["sell:plate:brassmouth"] }
  • A new shop opens: a "To let" lot becomes a front, or the quay grows by one front at the end.
  • The wharf closes for repairs: state = "shuttered". Shutters come down over the front with a chalked sign; clicking it gets the keeper's line about where to go instead.
  • A room goes away: it becomes a lot for a few dockings (counted, never timed), so the change is visible, then the quay closes up.
  • Validator: the section exists, the order is unambiguous, the quay length stays under the hub cap, and there is a keeper with a line citing the cause. No station mesh changes are needed, so the art cost of a new room is one awning and one interior.

4. Art direction

5. Hullwright fit

  • The wharf is a double front on the quay. Inside, the dry dock is wide enough to show the ship large with every socket at once.
  • Parts live on the rack: the Ready parts hang on pegs, Make parts show as outlines with what they need, Friends parts as a note pinned to an empty peg.
  • Neighbouring rooms feed it: the chandlery next door sells plate, and a drafting office (later) trades blueprints. The street makes these relationships visible.
  • Places differ by data: another station's wharf can be a single front with a short basin that only takes small parts, or a two-basin yard at a hub.

6. Risks, and what to test first

  • It can read as a menu dressed as a street. The quay is not the station's real shape, so the station you flew to and the place you walk can feel unrelated.
  • Sideways scrolling is awkward on many mice; drag and section pills must carry it.
  • Wide rooms on narrow screens. Every room interior must lay out at full width and at two-thirds height.
  • Test first: whether a two-front quay at the Wharf feels roomy rather than empty, and how long it takes to find a named room on a 14-front quay.

C. The harbour board

Docking pins up the station's board. Each part is a card with its keeper's face and one line of news; click a card and it unfolds into the room.

1. Structure

  • The station stays in view on the left, with your ship moored. On the right is a timber-framed cork board, and every part is a card pinned to it: a small sketch of the room, the keeper's portrait, and one line ("Orrin: your lens is ready to fit").
  • Click a card and it unfolds to fill most of the board. The other cards fold into a column at the side, still clickable. "Fold up" or right-click returns to the board.
  • Cards for parts that changed since your last docking carry a ribbon (New, Changed, Closed). This is the "what changed" at a glance, without a list of text.

2. Growth

  • Cards sit in three columns: Trade, Ship, People and news.
  • Outpost: two or three big cards with room for a sketch and two lines each.
  • Hub: up to four cards per column at a compact size; beyond that, a column's extra cards stack, and the stack fans out on click. Card sizes come in three steps (large, standard, compact), chosen from the count.
  • Pinned order is data, so the game master can put a new shop at the top of its column for a few dockings.

3. Game master hooks

The smallest data shape of the three, because a card has no place in the station's geometry.

# One card on a station's board.
[[station.cinder_hook.card]]
id       = "card.hook.rigger"
room     = "wharf"               # room kind the card unfolds into
column   = "ship"                # trade | ship | people
pin      = 1                     # order within the column
keeper   = "npc.rook_adeyemi"
headline = "Rook rigs drill mounts. Nothing fancy."   # one line, checked for length
state    = "open"                # open | closed | building
ribbon   = "new"                 # new | changed | closed, cleared after 3 dockings
works_on = ["drill"]             # this wharf only fits drill mounts
  • A new shop opens: add a card with ribbon = "new".
  • The wharf closes for repairs: state = "closed": the card is crossed with a strip of tape and the headline becomes the keeper's reason.
  • A part is upgraded: the card's sketch and headline change and it gets the "Changed" ribbon.
  • Validator: one-line headline, a keeper with a line citing the cause, the column cap. A visible change on the station itself is optional, so the "place" half of the legibility rule needs a separate asset-kit part.

4. Art direction

5. Hullwright fit

  • The drafting sheet is the fitting surface; the cradle beside the board is where the result shows. You drag on paper and watch the crane do it on the station.
  • Ready parts are cut-outs, Make parts are cut-outs with their materials pencilled on, and Friends parts are letters from the person who will send them.
  • The budget strip and stat preview run along the bottom of the sheet.
  • Places differ by data, and the sheet makes it obvious: a station's sheet shows only the sockets its wharf works on, with the rest drawn in faint pencil.

6. Risks, and what to test first

  • It is the closest to today's panel. Prettier, but still a menu; it may not fix "cramped" so much as tidy it.
  • Weakest sense of place. The station on the left is decoration unless every card also has a visible part on the station.
  • Headlines become a wall of text if the game master writes long ones, which is exactly what the log must avoid. The one-line limit has to be enforced.
  • Test first: show the Wharf board and today's panel to a playtester and ask which feels roomier; then fill a hub board to 13 cards and check it still reads at a glance.

Recommendation, agreed in review: A, the station up close. It is the only proposal where what the game master changes is what the player sees, which is the point of a living station: a new canteen is a dome going up on the pier, a closed wharf is scaffolding on the cradle arm. It reuses things that already exist (the runtime asset kit, the art bible's part budget, Kettle Dock's staged growth) instead of adding a second layout for stations. Its hullwright is the strongest version of the decided "wharf and drydock" feel.

Borrow from C: each icon in A's station key carries C's ribbon (New, Changed, Closed) and a one-line headline on hover. That gives the at-a-glance news without a board.

Keep from B for later: the "To let" lot is a good way to show a station has room to grow; in A it is an empty anchor with a marked plot.

What to test first, in order

  1. The hullwright cradle view at Lantern Wharf: drag to fit, the socket highlight, the stat preview and the one-and-a-half-second animation. Measure how long a first-time player takes to fit the lens, and whether they read the preview before letting go.
  2. The station overview at two sizes: the Wharf with 3 to 5 parts and a mock hub with 12 to 14 in quarters. Measure time to reach a named room and misclicks, with and without the station key.
  3. One game master change end to end: the wharf closes for repairs and reopens, with the scaffold, the key icon, Orrin's line and the rollback line, to prove a part change needs data only.

Dreaming ahead: a station that lives

Past the slice, the station becomes a place with its own life. Ships come and go around you, people work the pier, cranes carry real cargo, and every system builds and lights its stations in its own way. The camera never leaves the plane you fly on. It only tilts when you dock, so the station shows its height and depth.

This builds on A and C. The parts, the station key with news ribbons, the game master's data and the hullwright cradle stay as they are. What changes is how much the station shows of what is going on in it.

Semi-3D, without leaving the plane

  • Flight stays top-down. Docking swoops the camera in and tilts it to a three-quarter view, about 35 to 45 degrees from straight down, so module heights, fronts, awnings and cranes read. Undocking tilts back. The tilt is only a view: the ship and every craft still move on one plane, and there is no up or down to fly.
  • It is already 3D underneath. Stations are asset-kit meshes with ink outlines in a real 3D scene, so the tilt costs a camera move and some art that looks good from the side, not a new renderer.
  • Depth cues: far rock fields, lane mouths and clouds sit on parallax layers that drift slower than the station. Lamps are real point lights that pool on the decks. Modules cast soft shadows on the pier.
  • Room panels stay mouse-first, but each gets a live header: the market booth up close with Ilse at the counter, or the canteen with steam off the pots.
  1. A trader arriving is a real craft from the economy, heading for its berth (dashed). Its sale is about to move the price.
  2. The crane carries your ice from your berth to the stalls, because you just sold it.
  3. Crate piles show stock. They grow and shrink with the market, so you can see a glut or a shortage before you read a price.
  4. A small crowd at the notice wall means a new rumor or notice has landed.
  5. The lamplighter lights one more lamp at each station pulse, so the station's own clock is its traffic, never a timer.
  6. The canteen going up: scaffold, workers and welding sparks while the game master's part is "building".
  7. Your Wren at its berth, wearing its fitted parts.
  8. Your tender waiting in its standing berth for the price to climb, with its lamp lit.
  9. The owner's banner. During a dispute there are two, and after a handover the banner changes.
  10. A far layer of rocks and a lane mouth drifts slower than the station.
  11. The wharf at work on someone else's ship, with sparks under the gantry, so the hullwright is busy even when you are not fitting.

What makes it feel alive, and still calm

  • The station shows its simulation. Wherever possible, motion is a real thing happening: real craft, real cargo, real stock, real rumors. Decoration fills the gaps but never contradicts the numbers.
  • Driven by events, never by a clock. Bigger animations step on dockings, sales and station pulses, as the patient sell already does. Small idle loops (lamp flicker, steam, a banner moving) just run.
  • Gentle. Ambient motion is slow and lower in contrast than anything clickable. Nothing flashes for attention. A setting turns ambient life down for players who want a quiet dock.
  • A budget per station for walkers, craft and particles keeps frame time inside the scene's budget. Like every other feel number, it is measured from the first build that has it.

Theming: every system builds its own way

A theme is data: how a faction or a system builds, lights and peoples its stations. Every station picks one, and the game master can write new ones. A station's parts stay the same room kinds everywhere. The theme decides what they look like and what life goes on around them, so a market at a Compact exchange and a market at a Cooperative wharf work the same but never look alike.

# A theme: how a faction or system builds, lights and peoples its stations.
[theme.wharf_cooperative]
spine     = ["pier", "ring"]          # the layout the station grows along
motif     = "round"                   # from the art bible's faction entry
paint     = "faction.wharf_cooperative"
props     = ["lamp_string", "crate_pile", "greenhouse_glow", "steam"]
residents = ["dockhand", "lamplighter", "grower"]   # who walks the pier, and what they do
craft     = ["skiff", "barge"]        # filler traffic when no real craft is near
sounds    = ["kettle_whistle", "hammering", "market_talk"]
signage   = "lantern_kettle"
calm      = { max_walkers = 12, max_moving_craft = 4 }

# A happening: a short piece of visible life the game master writes.
[[happening]]
id      = "happening.wharf.lamp_night"
station = "station.lantern_wharf"
show    = ["banner_string:lamps", "crowd:pier.north", "residents:+6"]
lasts   = { dockings = 3 }            # counted, never timed
cause   = { facts = ["stage_built:kettle_dock:3"] }
line    = "line.ilse_marrow.lamp_night"
  • Themes keep generated stations coherent. When the game master grows a sixth station, it picks a theme, and the theme's spine and props decide where parts go and how they look. A new Lamplighter outpost looks like the Observatory's family without anyone drawing it.
  • Happenings are the "place" half of a change. A festival of lamps when Kettle's greenhouse finishes, barges queueing during a shortage, a crowd for a rumor. Each one is data with a cause and a line, and it runs for a few dockings.
  • The hullwright differs by place too. A Compact wharf fits square brass parts in strict order for a fixed fee per part, while a Cooperative cradle fits round timber parts and trades for favours. Parts can carry their maker's theme, so a well-travelled ship shows where it was fitted.

The hullwright, alive

  • In the tilted view the cradle has height: the ship is lifted off its berth, turns slowly, and the crane, the apprentices and the sparks are all in the scene.
  • Other cradles are busy with NPC ships, so the wharf is a working yard and not a shop that waits for you.
  • Fitting a part is the same drag as in A, played as a real lift: the part swings over, lowers and bolts on, and the ship flies out wearing it.

How it could grow, step by step

  1. Slice: A with C's ribbons, flat top-down, and the hullwright cradle view.
  2. Tilt and truth: the tilted docked camera, plus props driven by the simulation: crate piles from stock, banners, scaffold, the crane carrying your sales, and real NPC craft at the berths.
  3. People and sound: residents and work animations, sound beds, and one theme per faction.
  4. The game master's turn: themes and happenings as game master data, generated stations built from a theme's spine, and hullwrights that differ by system.

Risks, and what to test first

  • Busy against calm. Life must not turn into clutter. Keep motion slow and low in contrast, and give players a quiet setting.
  • Click targets among motion. Only modules and the key are clickable, and moving things never cover a sign.
  • Art and animation cost multiply by themes. The parametric kit and shared rigs for residents keep it in reach, and the game master's own asset work could help later.
  • The camera tilt between flight and dock can disorient. Keep it short, keep the ship centred, and let a click skip it.
  • Noise against meaning. If life is random, players learn to ignore it. Tie as much as possible to the simulation.
  • Test first (step 2): tilt the camera at Lantern Wharf, drive the crate piles from stock and the crane from the player's own sale, then ask playtesters whether the station feels more alive and whether they still find the wharf as fast. Report frame time from that first build.
